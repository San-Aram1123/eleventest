# 1. PROJECT BACKGROUND — AUDAR V19

The existing Audar V19 model uses:

- Qwen2 causal language model, approximately 0.55B parameters.
- NeuCodec acoustic tokens.
- 65,536 codec values.
- Approximately 50 codec frames per second.
- 24 kHz decoded audio.
- Reference-text and reference-audio conditioning.
- Nine learned emotional components.
- Four existing delivery modes.
- Nineteen signature presets.
- Discrete emotion intensities.
- Utterance-level emotion blending.

Existing active training-token dataset:

`datasets/tts/v18_clean_intro_20261011/tokens.jsonl`

Previously reported active partition: 10,460 clips.

Other reports mention 4,800 canonical rows. Reconcile these figures rather than assuming they refer to the same partition.

### V19's central limitation

V19 uses global emotional conditioning such as:

`[emotions=happy:medium,sad:medium; delivery=normal]`

Its training data lacks dedicated continuous recordings with labeled intra-utterance emotion changes.

V20 must introduce verified examples of:

- Neutral → Sad
- Sad → Angry
- Angry → Tender
- Fearful → Relieved
- Happy → Surprised → Anxious
- Multiple emotions blended simultaneously
- Gradual emotional changes
- Delivery switching within uninterrupted performances

Preserve the original V19 corpus and checkpoint.

Dataset production must remain separate from model architecture experimentation.

# 5. THE COMPLETE EMOTION TAXONOMY

The V20 target catalog contains 50 named emotional states.

All nine V19 emotions must remain backward compatible.

Every emotion needs:

- Canonical key.
- English description.
- Natural Sorani equivalent verified by a native linguist.
- Acoustic performance guidance.
- Low/medium/high-intensity script templates.
- Positive and negative test cases.
- Contrastive examples against acoustically similar categories.
- Human validation criteria.
- Known provider limitations.
- Multilingual support status.

Descriptions below are acoustic guidance, not fixed physical rules.

## E01 — Neutral

**Tag:** `[neutral]`

Emotionally balanced conversational speech.

Expected delivery:
- Natural cadence.
- Relaxed articulation.
- Moderate pitch variation.
- No exaggerated emotional coloring.

Example: "I arrived early and waited near the entrance."

Distinguish neutral from boredom and serenity.

## E02 — Happy

**Tag:** `[happy]`

Pleasant, positive emotional state.

Expected delivery:
- Warm vocal expression.
- Smiling articulation when natural.
- Lively but controlled prosody.

Example: "I'm really happy that we finally met again."

Differentiate from excitement and joy.

## E03 — Sad

**Tag:** `[sad]`

Sorrow, emotional pain, or disappointment.

Expected delivery:
- Restrained energy.
- Melancholic phrasing.
- Naturally softened emphasis.

Example: "I thought you would come back, but you never did."

Avoid forcing all sadness to sound breathy or slow.

## E04 — Angry

**Tag:** `[angry]`

Anger or confrontation.

Expected delivery:
- Tense articulation.
- Forceful emphasis.
- Strong intentional stress.

Example: "You knew the truth, and you still lied to me!"

Include both controlled anger and explosive anger.

## E05 — Excited

**Tag:** `[excited]`

High emotional arousal, usually positive.

Expected delivery:
- Energetic rhythm.
- Expressive pitch movement.
- Increased enthusiasm.

Example: "We did it! This is unbelievable!"

Do not equate excitement exclusively with rapid speech.

## E06 — Tender

**Tag:** `[tender]`

Gentleness and care.

Expected delivery:
- Warmth.
- Relaxed articulation.
- Soft emotional emphasis.

Example: "It's okay. You don't need to be afraid."

Distinguish tenderness from whispering.

## E07 — Curious

**Tag:** `[curious]`

Genuine interest or inquiry.

Expected delivery:
- Exploratory intonation.
- Attentive rhythm.
- Questioning emphasis.

Example: "How did you manage to solve that problem?"

## E08 — Sarcastic

**Tag:** `[sarcastic]`

Irony, mockery, or meaning contrary to literal wording.

Expected delivery:
- Deliberate emphasis.
- Context-dependent timing.
- Ironic pitch contours.

Example: "Oh, wonderful. Another excellent idea that failed."

The intended meaning must be clear from context.

## E09 — Fearful

**Tag:** `[fearful]`

Fear or apprehension.

Expected delivery:
- Heightened tension.
- Uncertain pacing.
- Possible breath irregularity.

Example: "Did you hear that? I think somebody is outside."

## E10 — Disgusted

**Tag:** `[disgusted]`

Repulsion or revulsion.

Expected delivery:
- Dismissive intonation.
- Expressive rejection.
- Tense articulation when natural.

Example: "That's disgusting. Please take it away."

## E11 — Surprised

**Tag:** `[surprised]`

Unexpected reaction to new information.

Expected delivery:
- Sudden intonation change.
- Expressive onset.
- Variable intensity.

Example: "Wait, you actually won?"

## E12 — Confused

**Tag:** `[confused]`

Difficulty understanding something.

Expected delivery:
- Thoughtful hesitation.
- Questioning tone.
- Uncertain rhythm.

Example: "I don't understand. Didn't you say the opposite?"

## E13 — Frustrated

**Tag:** `[frustrated]`

Irritation caused by repeated obstacles.

## E14 — Disappointed

**Tag:** `[disappointed]`

## E15 — Anxious

**Tag:** `[anxious]`

# 11. CONTINUOUS MULTI-EMOTION GENERATION — THE MOST IMPORTANT PART

## Absolute rule

A continuous multi-emotion dataset recording must be one genuine uninterrupted performance generated by the source TTS system.

Do not concatenate independently generated emotional WAV files and describe the result as continuous.

Do not confuse simultaneous emotion blending with sequential emotion switching.

Do not assume that a source model generated the requested transition just because its prompt contained tags.

Store both intended and verified emotional regions.

## C01 — Two-emotion switching

Example:

`[neutral] I thought everything would be okay, [sad] but nothing happened the way I expected.`

Target behavior:

- Begins naturally in neutral speech.
- Transitions into sadness.
- Preserves one voice.
- Maintains linguistic flow.
- No unnatural silence at the boundary.

Create many emotional pairs.

Include neutral-to-emotion, emotion-to-neutral, and emotion-to-different-emotion transitions.

## C02 — Three-emotion switching

Example:

`[happy] I finally received the letter, [surprised] but I never expected that name, [fearful] and suddenly I understood what it meant.`

Target:

Three distinct emotional regions in one uninterrupted recording.

## C03 — Four-to-five emotion switching

Example:

`[neutral] I opened the door, [curious] wondering who had arrived, [surprised] and suddenly I recognized you, [sad] after all these years, [happy] I could finally smile again.`

Target:

Multiple distinct transitions without speaker reset.

Use narrative situations that naturally justify the emotional progression.

## C04 — Within-sentence transitions

Example:

`[tender] I wanted to believe you, [sad] even after everything that happened, [angry] but you betrayed me again.`

This must be produced as one performance.

Emotion changes may occur within a single grammatical sentence.

Do not require a sentence-ending punctuation mark between emotional regions.

## C05 — Gradual transitions

Example emotional trajectory:

Neutral → Uneasy → Anxious → Fearful → Panicked.

The source recording should demonstrate an audible progression rather than a sudden switch.

Annotate the intended change as a time-varying emotional curve.

Store verified audible onset, peak, and transition regions when supported by reliable review.

A smooth mathematical annotation curve does not guarantee smooth acoustic speech.

## C06 — Simultaneous blends changing across time

Example:

Beginning: Happy 0.7 + Sad 0.3.

Middle: Sad 0.8 + Tender 0.4.

Ending: Relieved 0.9.

All regions must belong to the same uninterrupted acoustic performance.

## C07 — Delivery changes

Example:

Normal → Whisper → Normal.

Example:

Happy speech → Laughing speech → Happy speech.

Example:

Sad speech → Crying speech → Tender speech.

Keep emotion and delivery annotation tracks separate.

## C08 — Actions integrated into speech

# 17. NEUCODEC TOKENIZATION AND FRAME ALIGNMENT

Use the existing production NeuCodec tokenizer.

Preserve the verified codec configuration.

Expected nominal rate:

50 acoustic frames per second.

Frame duration:

20 milliseconds.

Map time annotations into codec frame indices only after successful audio alignment and tokenization.

Conceptual mapping:

`frame_index ≈ round(time_seconds × 50)`

But verify against actual encoded frame counts and encoder timing conventions.

Use half-open frame intervals:

`[start_frame, end_frame)`

Validate:

- Code vocabulary range.
- Frame count consistency.
- Audio duration.
- Boundary monotonicity.
- Timeline coverage.
- No invalid token IDs.
- Valid speaker information.
- Accurate normalized text.
- Correct source metadata.

Do not use repeating dummy token arrays as evidence of real codec compatibility.

Codec round-trip tests must use genuine audio and genuine encoded tokens.

# 18. SYNTHETIC AUDIO QUALITY CONTROL

Every candidate recording passes multiple stages.

## Gate A — Structural validation

- Valid WAV.
- Supported sample rate.
- Correct channel count.
- Valid file duration.
- No corruption.
- No empty recording.
- Valid metadata.

## Gate B — Text correspondence

- Speech matches the intended transcript.
- No hallucinated words.
- No dropped clauses.
- No major substitutions.
- Language matches declared language.

Use ASR as a screening tool, not the sole authority.

## Gate C — Acoustic quality

- No clipping.
- No severe distortion.
- No excessive silence.
- No trailing codec noise.
- No unnatural truncation.
- No repetitive audio loops.
- No artificial joins.

## Gate D — Expressive accuracy

- Intended emotion is audible.
- Emotional intensity is plausible.
- Delivery is appropriate.
- Continuous transitions are present.
- Nonverbal actions sound genuine.

## Gate E — Speaker stability

- Speaker identity remains consistent.
- No unintended voice changes.
- No sudden accent drift.
- No artificial speaker switching.

## Gate F — Human validation

Have native Sorani listeners review:

- Pronunciation.
- Grammar.
- Naturalness.
- Emotion.
- Emotion-transition quality.
- Speaker consistency.

Use independent ratings and retain disagreement information.

Do not impose universal acoustic rules such as every angry recording requiring higher RMS than neutral speech.

Calibrate measurable thresholds using real recordings and listener judgments.

# 19. DEDUPLICATION AND DATA LEAKAGE

Build deterministic script IDs from normalized text.

The same transcript generated by Gemini and ElevenLabs must map to a shared script identity.

Use speaker-specific family IDs separately.

Apply additional protection:

- Near-duplicate script detection.
- Shared original story grouping.
- Rewritten-text family grouping.
- Audio fingerprinting.
- Speaker leakage checks.
- Voice-clone lineage checks where applicable.

Never place the same or substantially duplicated script in both training and evaluation splits.

Support separate evaluation designs for:

- Unseen scripts.
- Unseen voices.
- Unseen emotion combinations.
- Unseen transition patterns.
- Unseen longer narratives.

Do not overstate perfect leakage prevention from exact text hashing alone.

# 20. DATASET SPLITTING

Use deterministic group-aware splitting.

Recommended initial proportions:

- Train: approximately 80%.
- Validation: approximately 10%.
- Test: approximately 10%.

These are starting points, not requirements that override strict leakage prevention.

Preserve a separate **human-recorded Sorani evaluation benchmark**.

Do not rely exclusively on synthetic voices to measure naturalness.

For speaker-independent evaluation, hold out entire speaker/voice identities.

For provider generalization, run source-specific evaluations.

Avoid comparing only matched scripts spoken by the same source voices.

# 21. LONG-FORM RECORDING POLICY

The V19 training pipeline previously constrained clips to approximately 20 seconds.

V20 should support longer continuous recordings after model-side sequence-length testing.

Create separate duration buckets:

- Short: 3–10 seconds.
- Medium: 10–20 seconds.
- Extended: 20–40 seconds.
- Long: 40–60 seconds.
- Experimental: 60–90 seconds.

Do not force all categories into the longest duration bucket.

Record the true acoustic duration and encoded frame count.

For long continuous speech, inspect:

- Pronunciation deterioration.
- Repetition.
- Word omissions.
- Speaker drift.
- Emotion resets.
- Excessive pauses.
- Unexpected ending.
- Nonverbal hallucinations.

Only enable training duration buckets that the selected V20 model architecture and hardware can support.
